I am a Ph.D. candidate at Princeton University’s School of Public and International Affairs, specializing in International Relations and Security Studies. My research examines how international pressures shape the strategic choices of states and the resilience of their political institutions. I work across two connected areas: nuclear security and coercive diplomacy, and the international forces that sustain or erode democratic governance.

**Nuclear security and coercive diplomacy.** My dissertation, *The Politics of Nuclear Prevention: Strategic Choices in Proliferation Crises*, explains why the United States and its allies choose diplomacy, sanctions, coercion, or military force when confronting would-be proliferators. It draws on cases of Iran, North Korea, Iraq, and Libya. The project combines original datasets and statistical analysis with archival research conducted at the British National Archives, the British Library, LSE Archives, and the Churchill Archives Centre.

**International forces and democratic resilience.** A second line of my research asks how external actors, including great-power coercion, sanctions regimes, international organizations, and international legal institutions, affect whether democratic institutions survive, deepen, or break down. The proliferation crises I study were often also moments when outside pressure reshaped domestic political order, from regime change in Iraq to state collapse in Libya. This agenda builds on my earlier research at the University of Chicago on international organizations, statebuilding, democratic institutions, and comparative international law. It also draws on my work as a research assistant in comparative politics at the University of Texas at San Antonio, studying the politics of developing countries, particularly regime durability and institutions in Singapore and Malaysia.

Across both areas, I integrate quantitative and qualitative methods: building original datasets, regression analysis, and theory-driven comparison. I am particularly interested in drawing credible inferences from imperfect data through small-N designs, careful case selection, and empirically grounded research strategies. I am committed to research that speaks to policy debates on nonproliferation, the use of force, and democratic resilience.

My work has been presented at Uppsala University’s Alva Myrdal Centre for Nuclear Disarmament, the Midwest Political Science Association Annual Conference, Princeton’s Center for International Security Studies, Yale’s MacMillan Center for International and Area Studies, and the University of Chicago’s International Relations Conference, among other venues. During the 2025–26 academic year, I was a Resident Fellow in the Nuclear Security Program at Yale’s MacMillan Center, and I was previously a Graduate Fellow at Princeton’s Center for International Security Studies (2022–2025). My research has been supported by Princeton PIIRS, the Princeton School of Public and International Affairs, and the Liechtenstein Institute on Self-Determination, among others.
